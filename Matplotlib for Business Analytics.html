Fig. 1

Why visualize data?

What

Data visualization encodes numbers as visual properties such as position, length, angle, and color, so the eye can compare values instead of reading them one at a time.

Why

Summary statistics can hide the shape of data. People spot trends, outliers, and gaps in a chart far faster than in a table, and in business the chart is often the only part of your analysis a decision maker sees.

How

Plot before you conclude. Explore with quick charts while analyzing, then build one polished chart per message when you present.

The classic proof: Anscombe's quartet

In 1973 statistician Francis Anscombe built four small datasets. Run the cell and look at the printed statistics first, then at the charts.

In [ ]: 
# Anscombe's quartet: four datasets with (almost) identical statistics
x  = np.array([10, 8, 13, 9, 11, 14, 6, 4, 12, 7, 5])

    'II':  np.array([9.14, 8.14, 8.74, 8.77, 9.26, 8.10, 6.13, 3.10, 9.13, 7.26, 4.74]),
    'III': np.array([7.46, 6.77, 12.74, 7.11, 7.81, 8.84, 6.08, 5.39, 8.15, 6.42, 5.73]),
    'IV':  np.array([6.58, 5.76, 7.71, 8.84, 8.47, 7.04, 5.25, 12.50, 5.56, 7.91, 6.89]),
}

fig, axes = plt.subplots(1, 4, figsize=(12, 3), sharey=True)
for ax, (name, y) in zip(axes, ys.items()):

          f"std y = {y.std(ddof=1):.2f}, corr = {np.corrcoef(xs, y)[0, 1]:.2f}")
    ax.scatter(xs, y, color='tab:blue')
    ax.set_title(f'Dataset {name}')

Where charts fit in an application

Recall the framework from the start of the course: Application = Data + Process + User Interface. NumPy and pandas handle data and processing. Matplotlib is the interface layer for analytics: it is where processed data becomes something a person can act on.

Fig. 2

Meet Matplotlib

What

Matplotlib is Python's foundational plotting library, created by John Hunter in 2003. It draws static, publication-quality 2D charts: lines, bars, histograms, scatter plots, and many more. Most everyday work goes through its pyplot module.

Why
  • It accepts NumPy arrays, lists, and pandas Series directly.
  • pandas .plot() and seaborn are built on top of it, so learning it lets you customize their charts too.
  • It controls every pixel: titles, colors, labels, annotations, layout.
  • It exports to PNG for slides and PDF or SVG for reports.
How

Import pyplot with the universal alias plt. In Jupyter, charts appear directly below the cell. Matplotlib comes pre-installed with Anaconda.

Setup: the data used throughout this tutorial

Run this cell once at the top of your notebook. It creates monthly revenue and costs as NumPy arrays, a pandas DataFrame and Series, and two simulated datasets of orders and stores. The random generator is seeded, so your numbers will match the ones shown here.

units   = np.array([820, 860, 950, 900, 1010, 1080,

# The same data as a pandas DataFrame

# Annual revenue by region ($K) as a pandas Series

stores['sales'] = (35 + 6.5 * stores['ad_spend']

Fig. 3

Anatomy of a figure

What

Every Matplotlib chart is a tree of objects. The Figure is the whole canvas. It contains one or more Axes, and each Axes holds the lines, bars, labels, ticks, and legend.

Why

Once you know each part's name, you know which method changes it. Almost every customization question becomes "which object, which set_ method?"

How

Click a part name or the chart itself to see what it is and the code that controls it.

Explore the parts of a chart

Axes is not axis. An Axes (plural-looking, but one object) is a whole plot area. An axis is one number line inside it, the x-axis or the y-axis, with its ticks and tick labels. fig, ax = plt.subplots() gives you one Axes named ax.

Fig. 4

Two ways to plot: pyplot and object-oriented

What

Matplotlib has two interfaces. The pyplot interface calls functions on plt and lets Matplotlib track the "current" chart for you. The object-oriented (OO) interface creates Figure and Axes objects and calls methods on them.

Why

pyplot is quick for a throwaway look at data. The OO interface is explicit about which chart you are changing, which matters as soon as you have more than one Axes. It is the style used in the Matplotlib documentation and in this course, and it connects to what you learned about objects and methods.

How

Start almost every chart with fig, ax = plt.subplots(), then call ax. methods.

The quickest possible chart (pyplot style)

The five-step recipe for every chart

  1. Prepare the data with NumPy or pandas: filter, aggregate, sort.
  2. Create the canvas: fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(8, 4))
  3. Draw the data: ax.plot(), ax.bar(), ax.hist(), ax.scatter(), and so on.
  4. Customize and label: title, axis labels, colors, legend, annotations.
  5. Show or save: plt.show() or fig.savefig('chart.png')

Choosing the right chart

What

Each chart type is built for one kind of comparison: change over time, ranking across categories, spread of values, relationship between two measures, or parts of a whole.

Why

The wrong chart makes the reader work to find the answer, or hides it. The right one makes the answer visible at a glance.

How

Write down the business question first. Then pick the chart whose job matches the question.

Design principle: question first, chart second

A chart answers a question. If you cannot say the question in one sentence, you are not ready to choose a chart yet.

Fig. 6

Line charts: showing trends over time

What

A line chart connects values in order, usually time on the x-axis. ax.plot(x, y) draws it.

Why

The connecting line shows direction and rate of change: growth, seasonality, dips, and turning points. Monthly revenue, weekly web traffic, and daily stock prices are all line-chart questions.

How

Call ax.plot() once per series. Give each a label= so ax.legend() can name it, and use color, marker, and linestyle to tell them apart.

Two series, a shaded margin, and a title that states the finding

In [ ]: 
fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(8, 4))
ax.plot(months, revenue, marker='o', color='tab:blue', label='Revenue')
ax.plot(months, costs, marker='s', linestyle='--', color='tab:gray',
        label='Costs')
ax.fill_between(months, costs, revenue, color='tab:blue', alpha=0.1)

ax.set_title('Revenue pulls away from costs in Q4', loc='left')
ax.set_ylabel('$K')
ax.legend(frameon=False)
ax.grid(axis='y', alpha=0.3)
ax.spines[['top', 'right']].set_visible(False)
plt.show()
Out:

fill_between() shades the area between two lines, which here makes the gross margin visible. Costs use a dashed gray line so revenue stays the visual focus.

Design principles for line charts

Put time on the x-axis, running left to right. Keep to about four lines or fewer; beyond that the chart turns into spaghetti. Use gray for context series and one strong color for the series you are talking about. A line's y-axis does not have to start at zero, because the reader judges slope, not length, but say so if you zoom in.

Fig. 7

Bar charts: comparing categories

What

A bar chart shows one value per category as the length of a bar. ax.bar() draws vertical bars; ax.barh() draws horizontal ones.

Why

People compare lengths that share a baseline very accurately, so bars are the most reliable way to answer "which is biggest?" and "by how much?" for regions, products, or sales reps.

How

Pass category names and values: ax.bar(series.index, series.values). Sort the data first with sort_values() so the ranking is visible.

A basic bar chart straight from a pandas Series

fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(7, 4))

ax.set_title('Revenue by Region')
ax.set_ylabel('Revenue ($K)')
plt.show()

Sorted, horizontal, directly labeled, one highlighted bar

In [ ]: 
ranked = regions.sort_values()   # smallest first, so the largest ends up on top
colors = ['tab:orange' if r == 'North' else 'lightgray' for r in ranked.index]

fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(7, 3.5))
bars = ax.barh(ranked.index, ranked.values, color=colors)
ax.bar_label(bars, fmt='$%dK', padding=3)          # label bars directly
ax.set_title('North trails every other region by at least $29K', loc='left')
ax.spines[['top', 'right', 'bottom']].set_visible(False)
ax.set_xticks([])                                  # labels make the axis redundant
plt.show()
Out:

Four deliberate changes: sort_values() turns the chart into a ranking; barh leaves room for long category names; bar_label() prints values on the bars so the x-axis can go; and the gray-plus-orange color scheme points at the one region the title is about.

Why sort ascending? barh draws the first item at the bottom. Sorting from smallest to largest therefore puts the largest bar on top, where people start reading.

Design principles for bar charts

Always start the value axis at zero: a bar's length is the data, so cutting the axis distorts every comparison (Section 12 shows how badly). Sort bars by value unless the categories have a natural order, such as months or age groups. Use horizontal bars when labels are long or there are many categories.

Distributions: histograms and box plots

What

A histogram splits one numeric column into ranges (bins) and counts how many values fall in each. A box plot summarizes the same spread with five numbers: minimum, first quartile, median, third quartile, and maximum, plus outlier dots.

Why

An average alone hides the shape of the data. Is a typical order $70, or are most orders $40 with a few $300 ones? Distributions answer questions about typical values, skew, and outliers, which drive pricing, inventory, and fraud checks.

How

ax.hist(values, bins=30) for one distribution; ax.boxplot([group1, group2, ...]) to compare distributions side by side.

Histogram with the mean and median marked

In [ ]: 
values = orders['order_value']
print(f"mean = {values.mean():.2f}, median = {values.median():.2f}")

fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(8, 4))
ax.hist(values, bins=30, color='tab:blue', edgecolor='white')
ax.axvline(values.median(), color='tab:orange', linestyle='--',
           label=f'Median  {values.median():.0f}')
ax.axvline(values.mean(), color='tab:red', linestyle=':',
           label=f'Mean  {values.mean():.0f}')
ax.set_title('Order values are right-skewed: a few big orders pull the mean up',
             loc='left')
ax.set_xlabel('Order value ($)')
ax.set_ylabel('Number of orders')
ax.legend(frameon=False)
plt.show()
Out:
mean = 74.23, median = 66.80

The mean ($74) sits to the right of the median ($67) because a long right tail of large orders pulls it up. For skewed business data such as order values, incomes, or house prices, the median usually describes a "typical" customer better.

Histogram or bar chart? They look alike but answer different questions. A bar chart has one bar per category. A histogram has one bar per numeric range, and the bars touch because the ranges are continuous.

Comparing groups with a box plot

In [ ]: 
channels = ['Online', 'Store', 'Phone']
data = [orders.loc[orders['channel'] == c, 'order_value'] for c in channels]

fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(7, 4))
ax.boxplot(data)
ax.set_xticks([1, 2, 3], channels)
ax.set_title('Order value by channel', loc='left')
ax.set_ylabel('Order value ($)')
plt.show()
Out:

The three channels have nearly identical boxes: order size does not depend much on channel. That is a real finding. Section 14 confirms it with groupby.

Design principle: bins change the story

Too few bins hide the shape; too many turn it into noise. Try a few values of bins (10, 30, 50) and keep the one that shows the pattern honestly without inventing spikes.

Fig. 9

Scatter plots: relationships between two measures

What

A scatter plot draws one dot per observation, with one measure on each axis. ax.scatter(x, y) draws it.

Why

It shows whether two measures move together, how strongly, and which observations break the pattern: ad spend and sales, price and units sold, tenure and salary. It is the visual first step before regression and machine learning.

How

Pass two equal-length columns. Use alpha (0 to 1) for transparency when dots overlap, and add a trend line with NumPy's np.polyfit().

In [ ]: 
x, y = stores['ad_spend'], stores['sales']
slope, intercept = np.polyfit(x, y, 1)          # straight-line fit
print(f"slope = {slope:.2f}, corr = {x.corr(y):.2f}")

line_x = np.linspace(x.min(), x.max(), 100)

fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(7, 4.5))
ax.scatter(x, y, alpha=0.7, label='Store')
ax.plot(line_x, slope * line_x + intercept, color='tab:orange',
        label=f'Trend: +{slope:.1f}K sales per 1K of ads')
ax.set_title('Stores that advertise more tend to sell more', loc='left')
ax.set_xlabel('Ad spend ($K)')
ax.set_ylabel('Sales ($K)')
ax.legend(frameon=False)
plt.show()
Out:
slope = 6.57, corr = 0.94

The slope of 6.57 means each extra $1K of ad spend goes with about $6.6K more sales, and a correlation of 0.94 means the dots hug the line closely. Notice how NumPy (polyfit, linspace) does the math and Matplotlib only draws the result: data structures and algorithms first, interface last.

Design principle: correlation is not causation

A scatter plot shows that two measures move together, not why. Bigger stores might both advertise more and sell more. Write titles that describe what the chart shows ("tend to sell more") rather than a cause you have not tested ("ads drive sales").

Composition: stacked bars and the pie problem

What

Composition charts show how a total splits into parts, such as revenue by product line or market share by company.

Why

Managers ask both "how big is the total?" and "what is it made of?" A stacked bar answers both, and shows how the mix changes over time.

How

With a DataFrame of one column per part, df.plot(kind='bar', stacked=True) builds it in one line. Use shades of one color so the parts read as one whole.

In [ ]: 
quarterly = pd.DataFrame(

ax.set_title('Software is the fastest-growing product line', loc='left')
ax.set_ylabel('Revenue ($K)')
ax.legend(title='Product line', frameon=False,
          bbox_to_anchor=(1.02, 1), loc='upper left')

Only the bottom segment (Hardware) sits on a common baseline, so it is the easiest to compare across quarters. Put the part you care most about at the bottom.

Why analysts avoid pie charts

The same market-share data as a pie and as a sorted bar

In [ ]: 
share = pd.Series({'Us': 24, 'Rival A': 22, 'Rival B': 19,
                   'Rival C': 15, 'Rival D': 12, 'Others': 8})

fig, (ax1, ax2) = plt.subplots(1, 2, figsize=(11, 4))

ax1.pie(share, labels=share.index, startangle=90, counterclock=False)
ax1.set_title('Pie: are we bigger than Rival A?')

ranked = share.sort_values()
colors = ['tab:orange' if name == 'Us' else 'lightgray' for name in ranked.index]
bars = ax2.barh(ranked.index, ranked.values, color=colors)
ax2.bar_label(bars, fmt='%d%%', padding=3)
ax2.set_title('Bar: yes, by 2 points')
ax2.spines[['top', 'right', 'bottom']].set_visible(False)
ax2.set_xticks([])
plt.tight_layout()
plt.show()
Out:

Humans judge angles and areas poorly. In the pie, 24% and 22% look the same; in the bar, the gap is obvious. The pie also needs six colors and a legend-like label hunt, while the bar needs one accent color.

Design principle: when a pie is acceptable

Use a pie only when there are two or three parts, the parts add up to a meaningful whole, and the message is roughly "about half" or "most." Never use 3D pies or exploded slices; the perspective distorts the areas.

Designing for clarity

What

Chart design is the set of choices beyond "draw the data": what to remove, what to color, what to label, and what the title says.

Why

Your audience gives a chart a few seconds. Every decoration that does not carry data, such as heavy gridlines, borders, and rainbow colors, competes for those seconds. Edward Tufte called the goal a high data-ink ratio: spend ink on data, not decoration.

How

Declutter, highlight one thing, label directly, and write an action title. The toggle below shows all four on the same data.

Use color with a purpose

Color should mean something. The gray-plus-accent pattern used throughout this tutorial works because gray says "context" and the accent says "look here." A few rules keep color honest and accessible:

SituationColor approachMatplotlib
One item matters mostGray for everything, one accentcolor=['lightgray', ..., 'tab:orange']
A few unordered categoriesDistinct hues (the default tab10 palette)'tab:blue', 'tab:orange', 'tab:green'

Accessibility. About 1 in 12 men has some form of color vision deficiency, most often red-green. Avoid red-versus-green as the only difference, and back up color with position, labels, or line style (as the dashed costs line does in Section 6).

Format numbers and annotate key points

In [ ]: 
from matplotlib.ticker import StrMethodFormatter

fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(8, 4))
ax.plot(months, revenue * 1000, marker='o', color='tab:blue')
ax.yaxis.set_major_formatter(StrMethodFormatter('${x:,.0f}'))   # 60000 -> $60,000
ax.set_ylim(0, 100_000)

ax.annotate('Holiday peak', xy=(11, 88_000), xytext=(7.2, 90_000),
            arrowprops=dict(arrowstyle='->', color='gray'))
ax.annotate('Summer dip', xy=(8, 58_000), xytext=(6.5, 35_000),
            arrowprops=dict(arrowstyle='->', color='gray'))

ax.set_title('Monthly revenue, 2026', loc='left')
ax.spines[['top', 'right']].set_visible(False)
plt.show()
Out:

StrMethodFormatter('${x:,.0f}') uses the same format mini-language as Python f-strings: , adds thousands separators and .0f drops decimals. annotate() places text at xytext with an arrow pointing to xy. On a categorical axis, x positions are the category index, so 11 means December.

Watch the dollar sign. Matplotlib treats text between two $ signs as a math formula. One $ in a label is fine; if a label needs two, escape them as \$, for example 'From \$40 to \$90'.

Fig. 12

Honest charts: axes that do not lie

What

A misleading chart is technically accurate but gives a false impression, most often through a truncated axis, a stretched scale, or two y-axes chosen to force a pattern.

Why

Business decisions such as budgets, bonuses, and layoffs get made from charts. An exaggerated difference can move money. Analysts are responsible for the impression a chart creates, not just its numbers.

How

Control axis ranges explicitly with ax.set_ylim(). Start bar charts at zero, and be very careful with twinx().

Dual y-axes with twinx()

Sometimes two measures with different units belong on the same timeline, such as units sold and average price. ax.twinx() creates a second Axes that shares the x-axis but has its own y-axis on the right.

In [ ]: 
avg_price = revenue * 1000 / units      # $ per unit

fig, ax1 = plt.subplots(figsize=(8, 4))
ax1.bar(months, units, color='lightgray')
ax1.set_ylabel('Units sold', color='gray')

ax2 = ax1.twinx()                        # second y-axis that shares the x-axis
ax2.plot(months, avg_price, color='tab:blue', marker='o')
ax2.set_ylabel('Average price ($)', color='tab:blue')

ax1.set_title('Volume and price both rose in Q4', loc='left')
plt.show()
Out:

Use with caution. Each y-axis scales independently, so the price line looks like it climbs far more steeply than volume, even though price rose about 19% and volume about 77%. Anyone can make two unrelated series appear to move together by choosing the two ranges. Color-code each axis to its series (as above), and consider two stacked subplots with sharex=True instead.

Subplots: building a small dashboard

What

plt.subplots(nrows, ncols) creates one Figure containing a grid of Axes. With more than one Axes, it returns them as a NumPy array.

Why

Related views side by side let a reader see the whole business at once: trend, ranking, distribution, relationship. Shared axes make panels directly comparable.

How

Index the array like any 2D NumPy array: axes[row, col]. Loop over axes.flat to style every panel. Finish with fig.tight_layout() so labels do not overlap.

In [ ]: 
fig, axes = plt.subplots(2, 2, figsize=(11, 7))   # a 2-row, 2-column grid

axes[0, 0].plot(months, revenue, marker='o')
axes[0, 0].set_title('Monthly revenue ($K)', loc='left')
axes[0, 0].tick_params(axis='x', labelrotation=45)

ranked = regions.sort_values()
axes[0, 1].barh(ranked.index, ranked.values, color='tab:blue')
axes[0, 1].set_title('Revenue by region ($K)', loc='left')

axes[1, 0].hist(orders['order_value'], bins=30, color='tab:blue', edgecolor='white')
axes[1, 0].set_title('Order value distribution ($)', loc='left')

axes[1, 1].scatter(stores['ad_spend'], stores['sales'], alpha=0.7)
axes[1, 1].set_title('Ad spend vs. sales ($K)', loc='left')

for ax in axes.flat:                                 # style every panel at once
    ax.spines[['top', 'right']].set_visible(False)

fig.suptitle('Sales Dashboard, 2026', fontsize=15, fontweight='bold')
fig.tight_layout()
plt.show()

Plotting directly from pandas

What

Every pandas Series and DataFrame has a .plot() method that calls Matplotlib for you and returns the Matplotlib Axes it drew on.

Why

It turns a groupby result into a chart in one line, using the index for labels and column names for the legend. Because it returns an Axes, everything in this tutorial still works afterward.

How

Choose the chart with kind= (or df.plot.bar()), capture the result as ax, then customize with ax.set_ methods.

DataFrame columns become lines; column names become the legend

In [ ]: 
ax = sales.plot(x='month', y=['revenue', 'costs'], marker='o',

ax.spines[['top', 'right']].set_visible(False)
plt.show()

Styles and saving your work

What

Style sheets change the default look of every chart (fonts, colors, backgrounds, grids) at once. savefig() writes a Figure to an image or document file.

Why

Styles give a report a consistent look without repeating code. Saved files are how charts leave the notebook and reach slides, reports, and dashboards.

How

Use plt.style.context('name') inside a with block for one chart, or plt.style.use('name') for the rest of the notebook. Save with fig.savefig() before plt.show().

In [ ]: 
print(plt.style.available[:6])

with plt.style.context('ggplot'):          # applies only inside this block
    fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(8, 4))
    ax.plot(months, revenue, marker='o')
    ax.set_title("Same chart, 'ggplot' style")
    plt.show()
